The universe is slowing down. Obviously. Right?

In 1998, two rival teams raced to measure how fast the universe is slowing down. The answer looked so wrong they spent months trying to kill it. It survived.

⏱ ~25 min 🔭 astronomy difficulty ●●○○ 🏅 nobel prize 2011

Riess et al. (1998) · “Observational Evidence from Supernovae for an Accelerating Universe and a Cosmological Constant” · The Astronomical Journal 116, 1009 · High-Z Supernova Search Team

act 0 your intuition, on the record

Throw a ball in the air.

It slows down. Gravity. Every thrown object in history has done this.

Now scale up. The universe has been expanding since the Big Bang. But it's full of stuff, and all that stuff pulls on all the other stuff.

So the expansion should be slowing. Like the ball.

The only open question was how much it's slowing. That number decides how everything ends. Two endings were on the menu:

act 1 the setup

A blunder, and two teams that didn't like each other

First, meet the villain of the story. In 1917, Einstein's own equations told him the universe couldn't sit still. He didn't believe them. So he bolted on a fudge factor — the — to hold everything in place.

concept · cosmological constant (Λ)

Energy belonging to empty space itself. Matter pulls things together; this pushes space apart. Einstein invented it to freeze the universe, then dropped it when expansion was discovered.

Twelve years later, Hubble showed the universe was expanding. The fudge factor got deleted. The story goes Einstein called it his biggest blunder.

Remember it. It comes back.

By the 1990s, two groups believed the slowdown could finally be measured. They just had to compare the expansion rate now with the rate billions of years ago.

Team 1 — the Supernova Cosmology Project. Saul Perlmutter's group at Berkeley. Particle physicists, outsiders, grinding since 1988 while astronomers rolled their eyes.

Team 2 — the High-Z Supernova Search Team. Formed 1994 by Brian Schmidt. Veteran supernova astronomers, including a postdoc named Adam Riess — whose analysis is this paper.

Same telescopes. Same goal. Real rivalry. Keep that in mind too, because it's about to matter in the best possible way.

act 2 the measurement problem

How do you measure five billion light-years?

No tape measure reaches another galaxy. But there's a trick you already know: a light bulb looks dimmer the farther away it is. Precisely, predictably dimmer.

So if you knew a bulb's true wattage, its apparent dimness would tell you its distance. Astronomers call such an object a .

concept · standard candle

Anything whose true brightness is known. Compare true brightness to apparent brightness and the dimming factor gives you distance — like judging how far a 60-watt bulb is at night.

One problem: the universe would have to sell a standardized bulb. It does.

When a steals gas from a neighboring star, its mass creeps up toward a hard physical ceiling. The moment it arrives, it detonates: a Type Ia supernova.

concept · white dwarf

The dead core of a Sun-like star — the Sun's mass crushed into an Earth-sized ball. Past 1.4 solar masses (the Chandrasekhar limit) it can't hold itself up, and it explodes.

Same trigger mass, every time. So: same explosion, same true brightness, every time. A standardized bomb, visible from halfway across the universe.

Second problem: these explosions are once-a-century events, and they fade in weeks. You can't point at a galaxy and wait.

So the teams stopped waiting for luck and started manufacturing it:

01 · imagePhotograph thousands of galaxies

Wide-field camera, moonless night. One frame holds ~5,000 galaxies.

02 · waitCome back ~3 weeks later

Long enough for new explosions. Short enough that none have faded.

03 · subtractNew image minus old image

Everything unchanged cancels to black. A leftover dot is new.

04 · confirmGrab its spectrum

Verify it's a Type Ia; measure its redshift — how much the universe stretched in transit.

05 · trackWatch it rise and fade

The fade rate (Phillips, 1993) fine-tunes true brightness. Now it's calibrated.

By late 1997 the High-Z team had a set of calibrated candles so distant their light left home when the universe was two-thirds its current age.

Riess's job: plot each one's against its dimness, and read off the slowdown.

concept · redshift

Expanding space stretches light to longer, redder wavelengths. A supernova's redshift records how much the universe grew while its light was traveling — a timestamp on the expansion.

The logic of the plot, in one breath: if expansion has been slowing, the universe covered ground fast early on, so light hasn't had to travel as far — distant supernovae should look a touch brighter than the naive line. If it never slowed: exactly on the line.

There was a third mathematical possibility. Nobody took it seriously.

◈ prediction checkpoint — commit before you scroll

January 1998. Riess runs the comparison. The distant supernovae come out…

The supernovae were too dim. Ten to fifteen percent farther than they had any right to be. The expansion isn't slowing. It's accelerating — the ball, thrown upward, falling up.

act 3 assume you screwed up

“Somewhere between amazement and horror”

That's Brian Schmidt describing his reaction. Not eureka. Horror.

Because when your measurement says the universe defies gravity, the likely explanation is that you made a mistake. And there was a second problem: pushed further, the math demanded something pushing space outward. The least-crazy candidate was Einstein's deleted fudge factor.

Junior scientists, about to resurrect the most famous blunder in physics, in public.

So they spent months attacking their own result. This is the part beginners never get shown, and it's the best part. Three suspects — for each one, guess the check before you reveal it.

SUSPECT №1Cosmic dust

Maybe the supernovae aren't far. Maybe they're just dimmed by dust in the way — headlights in fog.

Dust doesn't just dim light — it reddens it. Same reason sunsets are red. The distant supernovae showed no reddening at all. To survive, the dust would need to be an exotic "gray" kind that dims all colors equally, spread evenly across the whole cosmos, invented purely to make this one measurement wrong.

✓ suspect cleared
SUSPECT №2Ancient supernovae are different

These explosions happened billions of years ago, in younger galaxies with fewer heavy elements. Maybe old-recipe white dwarfs make dimmer bombs?

Nearby galaxies also come in young-ish and old-ish flavors. Compare supernovae across both — after the fade-rate correction, they match beautifully. No drift with age.

✓ suspect cleared
SUSPECT №3Their own pipeline

Software bugs, calibration errors, wishful statistics. How do you rule out yourself?

You can't, alone. But remember the rivals? Perlmutter's team — different telescope time, different software, every incentive to catch the High-Z team in an error — was quietly finding the same dimness in their own 42 supernovae. When competitors converge, the instruments stop being the suspect. The universe becomes the suspect.

✓ suspect cleared

Early 1998, Riess ran the formal statistics. Even with every check applied, the data insisted — above 99% confidence — on an accelerating universe with a nonzero cosmological constant.

The paper went out that spring. Perlmutter's 42 supernovae agreed the next year. Thirteen years later, the rivals flew to Stockholm together.

act 4 what it means

Mostly made of something we can't name

If expansion is accelerating, something is doing the pushing — some energy of empty space itself. Exactly the job Einstein once invented Λ for. Physicists gave the mystery a placeholder name: dark energy.

Then they did the bookkeeping on how much of it the acceleration requires. It's the most humbling accounting in science:

The paper didn't just change the universe's fate. It demoted everything we can see to a rounding error.

And about that bet you placed in Act 0 — the fate did change. Not a crunch. Not a coast. A third ending nobody ordered:

What is dark energy? Twenty-eight years later: still nobody knows. It's arguably the biggest open problem in physics.

And the story isn't closed. Since 2024, the DESI survey has seen hints — hints, not verdicts — that dark energy may be weakening over time. Which would break the simple picture all over again. The next act may already be in the data.

The skeptic's corner

A result this strange deserves suspicion. Each objection below is real. Each was answered.

QCouldn't it still be dust, but weirder dust?
There's a kill shot. Very early on, the universe was dense enough that expansion really was decelerating — so the most distant supernovae should flip to looking brighter than the naive line. In 2001–2004, Riess found them. They flipped. Dust can only ever dim; it can't bend the curve both ways.
QIsn't ~10 supernovae a tiny sample?
Yes — this paper leaned on 10 well-measured distant ones (16 total). That's why the rival team's independent 42 mattered so much. Today the samples number in the thousands and the signal has only sharpened.
QDoes anything besides supernovae agree?
The clincher. The cosmic microwave background and the clustering pattern of galaxies — two totally different measurements — independently demand the same ~70/25/5 budget. Three unrelated rulers, one answer.
QCould it be a statistical fluke?
The 1998 paper claimed >99% confidence — good, not overwhelming. But flukes fade as data grows. This signal grew. By the mid-2000s it had passed every reasonable bar, which is why Stockholm called in 2011.
